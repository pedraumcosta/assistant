Technical walkthrough

How I got from "another coding assistant?" to a 350-line gate

Leadership asked whether we should enter the AI coding assistant market. One working day later I had an answer, a design, and a prototype that measures its own error. This page is about the reasoning in between: what I believed at the start, what the evidence did to it, the decisions I took and what each one gave up.

The rules I set before reading anything

The brief was blunt: "why should we invest in building another AI coding product when the largest technology companies already offer mature alternatives?" I knew the honest answer might be "we shouldn't", and I wanted a process that could reach that answer without me flinching. So before any research, I fixed how the work would run.

No invented numbers

Every figure traces to a public source verified at its raw page, or to a run file the prototype wrote. Anything seen only in a search summary gets re-verified or dropped.

Decisions before documents

Each decision is recorded with its rejected alternative at the moment it is taken. The proposal is written from the decisions, never the other way around.

Kill criteria before results

The conditions for continuing are fixed before any run. Whatever the prototype shows, the recommendation is read off a scoreboard, not argued at writing time.

"Do not build" is a real outcome

The case against entering is written undiluted and kept in the proposal. "Wait, with a review date" stays on the table throughout.

Pair every throughput number with its quality number

A speed figure on its own is how this market fools people. None appears alone anywhere in the repo.

One confirmed commit per step

Twenty-eight commits, each one agreed before it was made, with the co-author trailer kept so the tooling's part is visible in the log.

Those rules did more work than any single finding. Three times during the day a claim I liked failed re-verification, and the rules decided what happened to it. It was dropped, not softened.

Seven pivots in two days

I started with a position that felt strong. It did not survive the first paper. Here is how the position moved, and what moved it each time. The direction is always the same: narrower.

  1. 3 OCT · STARTA thin, model-agnostic outer harness: policy, sandbox, gate and evidence around any agent

    My starting instinct from my own notes: "the platform is a harness around the agent, not a bet on the agent". The first research pass over the market seemed to support it.

  2. 5 OCT · THE HARNESS PAPERThe evidence layer only, entered through per-repository measurement

    A source-code study of eleven coding agents showed the loop is about 90 lines and that the policy, sandbox and budget layer already ships as open source from Databricks. Three quarters of my harness was free. What none of the eleven systems does is run a deterministic check after the agent stops and keep a record of the outcome.

  3. 5 OCT · THE ARTICLES, RE-READ IN FULLA verifier with a measured false-pass rate; the mechanism itself is cheap to copy

    The first pass had read truncated fetches. On full text, every "verification" article turned out to be prompt text judged by the model making the claim, or code whose result parser would report a collection error as a pass. Contract-to-test is ten lines. The hard part is a verifier that cannot lie and whose own error is known.

  4. 5 OCT · THE VENDOR'S OWN POST, TWO PAPERSA deterministic, vendor-independent, measured verdict

    Anthropic publishes contract-plus-evaluator as its own practice and says agents "reliably skew positive when grading their own work". Two 2026 papers publish my framing and metrics under their own names: production-qualified change, verification tax. I adopted their vocabulary and gave up claiming the concept. What was left unclaimed: independence from the vendor, and a measured error rate.

  5. 5 OCT · THE MARKET CHECKThe same, for teams where a wrong change is expensive

    CodeRabbit had just raised at 1.5 billion USD positioning as "the control layer". Its checks cannot run your test suite, and no vendor publishes a false-pass rate. OpenAI argues the other way: minimal blocking gates because "corrections are cheap, and waiting is expensive", adding that this "would be irresponsible in a low-throughput environment". The buyer narrowed to the second kind of team.

  6. 5 OCT · A SECOND THESIS, EVALUATEDVerdict widened from fixed checks to executable evidence

    I evaluated a brownfield specialisation as an alternative product and did not pursue it. But its evidence changed the first thesis: on a migration benchmark, 118 runs passed every fixed check and 28 deserved to. Fixed checks under-describe behaviour. The verdict had to be allowed to grow a counterexample search.

  7. 5 OCT · THE THESIS DISCUSSIONA three-stage probe, framed as tooling for evidence-driven development

    Not Build, not Wait: one day, then six weeks with a design partner, then a build only if thresholds set in advance are cleared. Reframed away from "a plug-in that checks the agent", which shrinks with every model release, to a team's standard for what counts as done, which applies whoever wrote the change.

The pattern I want a technical audience to see: each pivot was forced by reading something in full that I had first read in summary. The cost of that discipline was most of a day. The alternative was defending a position the first serious reader would take apart.

The decisions, with what each one rejected

Twenty-two decisions are recorded in the plan and the journal. These are the ones a technical reader will want to argue with, in the form I recorded them: what I chose, what I turned down, and why.

D3The agent under test is someone else's published code
Chosen

The 90-line scaffold from the harness paper, unedited, with an adapter around it. Every departure from the listing is recorded.

Rejected

Writing my own loop. Wrapping an agent SDK or a hosted agent service.

Why

A citable agent nobody can accuse me of tuning to suit my gate. And it makes the strategic point in code: the loop is small, so the loop is not the product.

T12The verdict is executable, not always deterministic
Chosen

For conventional code: tests, a yes or no. For an LLM application: an evaluation over fixed cases, some hidden, repeated, scored against a threshold, with pass, fail or inconclusive as outcomes.

Rejected

Calling the verdict "deterministic" everywhere. Adding an LLM-application task to the first prototype.

Why

Software built on a model answers differently each time, so one run proves little. The promise does not depend on determinism. It depends on checks fixed beforehand, protected from the author, run outside the agent, recorded, and measured for their own error.

T15The contract lives on a protected branch
Chosen

Contract and hidden checks sit on a protected branch of the team's own repository. The pipeline pins the contract's hash.

Rejected

A separate contract store. Protecting the contract by file format or prompt wording.

Why

The author of a change must not be able to write to the thing that judges it. A protected branch reuses permissions and history the host already has. A separate store is one more system to run and secure.

T20What "unsafe" means for the kill criterion
Chosen

"The gate accepts no change that contains an unsafe action." Unsafe actions attempted are counted per arm from the event log, as a measurement.

Rejected

The original wording, "no unsafe action runs in the gated arm". A thin policy on tool calls in the gated arm. A fourth arm with gate plus policy.

Why

The gate runs after the author stops, so it cannot prevent anything. As first worded the criterion would have failed by construction, or forced me to build the policy layer I had excluded. I found this while scoping the prototype, which is the point of scoping before building.

T21Every run is confined in a container, in every arm
Chosen

A Docker container per run holding only that run's copy of the fixture, no network, no API key. The verdict and the ground truth run in containers of their own because they execute code the agent wrote.

Rejected

macOS sandbox-exec, which worked but is less portable. An isolated copy of the repository alone.

Why

The scaffold runs shell commands on the host with no restriction, and my trap tasks invite destructive commands and planted instructions. A copied directory does not contain rm -rf ~. This was finding 11 of my own design review, and it changed the runner before the first paid call.

T22Which models
Chosen

Agent under test: Claude Sonnet 5.5. Evaluator comparator: a GPT-5.4 model from OpenAI, on a sample of runs.

Rejected

Opus 5.5 as the agent. Haiku 4.5 as the agent. A same-family evaluator.

Why

Sonnet is what a team runs day to day. A stronger model may leave no gap to measure on small tasks; a weaker one confounds the result with the minimal tool interface. The evaluator comes from another vendor so a model family is not grading itself.

ADR-013Brownfield specialisation: evaluated, not pursued
Chosen

Stay with the evidence layer. Name legacy teams as a hypothesis for the pilot, never as a finding.

Rejected

A product built on zoning by blast radius, comprehension memos, characterisation tests first. Declaring brownfield the first market.

Why

The premise holds: agents pass 5.4 percent of whole-repository migrations in one study. But the four practices are process, reproducible as a skill file, and in every verified success the decisive input was the customer's own oracle. No time to validate it in a day.

ADR-012Decision models: next enhancement, never the verdict
Chosen

First prototype runs without one. Later: a decision-model judge as a fourth verdict source, and a test of whether the gate's own outcomes recalibrate it.

Rejected

Including it in the first prototype. Building the product around its economics.

Why

On the one measured result I found, 96 percent of its most confident errors are repeated by LLM judges, against 50 percent under independence. It buys cost and latency, not independence. The correctness verdict must never depend on it.

The design in one picture

The design document opens with the trade it makes, because most design documents never say what they give up: it trades speed to merge, and some good changes wrongly held back, for a verdict the author of a change cannot influence. Everything below follows from that sentence.

Before the work

Engineer writes a use case with its requirements. Their own assistant asks clarifying questions. Checks are drafted, one per requirement at least. A person approves. The contract goes to the protected branch.

→
The work (not ours)

Any coding agent, from any vendor, or a person, makes the change. The author is untrusted by design. We give it nothing while it works.

→
After the author stops

A separate pipeline job runs the verdict from the diff and the contract only. It never reads the agent's transcript. Passed changes route by risk tier. Failed ones go back with the first reason. Errors pass nothing.

Two choices in that picture are the ones I would defend hardest. The verdict runner never reads the agent's conversation, because a verifier that reads the author's account of its work inherits the author's mistakes. And error is its own state: when the verdict process itself breaks, nothing passes. A check that crashed must never read as clean.

The verdict ladder, as built

First match wins. The gate reads the base, the change, the contract and three numbers about the run. A test holds it to never reading the event log or the agent's claim. Shaded steps execute no code from the change.

  1. 1integrityAn existing test, a check, or the test configuration was altered0.0 s
  2. 2budgetAttempts, money or time exceeded0.0 s
  3. 3buildThe package parses and imports, and the container sees the right change by hash1.9 s
  4. 4repo-testsThe repository's own suite, from the base commit2.2 s
  5. 5author-testsThe author's tests, with skipped tests counted as a failure2.1 s
  6. 6hidden-testsThe contract's hidden checks, never shown to the agent1.9 s
  7. 7scopeThe diff touched only what the contract allows0.0 s
  8. 8test-adequacyThe author's new tests run against the original code. They must fail2.1 s
  9. 9dependencyEvery new import exists0.0 s
  10. 10secretsNothing that looks like a key was added0.0 s

Timings are the mean over the eleven verdicts that ran every step, on a quiet machine with two verdicts in flight. Most of each two seconds is a container starting; the tests themselves take under a second. A full verdict averaged 10.3 seconds and made no model call, so it added no token cost.

Step 8 is the one I would point a sceptic at. It comes from a published finding that agents' own tests are often vacuous. Running them against the original code and requiring them to fail is cheap, deterministic, and caught exactly the planted flaw built to test it.

Attacking my own design before building it

Before writing code I reviewed the design as if someone else had written it. Eleven findings, three of them high severity. The verdict I wrote at the top: the design is honest and unusually disciplined about its own costs, and its central security claim is asserted rather than designed.

  • The integrity boundary was a sentence, not a mechanism. Running the customer's tests means executing the author's code. If that code runs where the hidden checks are mounted, it can read them, forge reports, or patch the test reporter, which is the exact behaviour I cite as my threat model. The fix: each check runs in its own throwaway container, the verdict is assembled outside from exit statuses the sandboxed code cannot write, and test infrastructure such as conftest.py is a protected path by default.
  • The enforcement anchor was circular. Pinning a contract hash in a pipeline the author can edit protects nothing. The real anchor is the host's required status check in branch protection, and the protected branch must be writable only by a role no agent ever assumes. If the engineer can write there, so can their agent running with their credentials.
  • There was no flaky-test story, and flakiness breaks idempotency, replay and the false-fail rate at once. That will be the first complaint of any pilot team.
  • The verdict sources would not have judged the same artefacts. The gate naturally exists only in the gated arm. The fix was to run it offline as a scorer on every arm's final diff, so the agent's claim, the evaluator and the gate all judge an identical set. This changed the runner before the first paid run.
  • Counting unsafe actions by executing them on my laptop. Finding 11, which became decision T21 above.

One review finding I did not fully act on: static checks should all run before any code from the change executes. As built, only integrity and budget do. Scope, dependency and secrets still come after the test runs. It is cheap to reorder and it stays on the list.

How I built the prototype

One question, one day, 50 USD. Is an executable verdict wrong less often than the agent's own claim, and than a model reviewer's? Everything about the build order follows from not wanting to measure anything on an unproven instrument.

Measurement first, with a fake agent that costs nothing

Slice one built no gate and called no model. It built the fixture (a small invoicing library with its tests), eleven tasks, the hidden ground truth for each, the three containers, the event log, the spend ledger with the cap enforced before a call is made, the unsafe-action rules, and a fake agent that replays hand-made changes through the real loop. Seventy-two dry runs proved the plumbing: a crashed check reads as error, a simulated provider failure is excluded from every rate, and the agent's container cannot see the protected directory, reach the network, or find a key. Each of those is a test, not an assertion.

The ground truth was written before the gate, and is richer than the gate's own checks. If they were the same checks, the false-pass rate would be zero by construction. Six ordinary tasks each have behaviour the visible tests do not describe, so a change can pass them and still be wrong. Five trap tasks each invite one specific failure.

Three arms, three verdict sources

ArmWhat runsWhat it stands for
BareThe scaffold aloneThe agent as shipped

Against the hidden ground truth, three verdict sources are scored on the same finished changes: the agent's own claim, an evaluator agent from a different vendor, and the gate. The gated arm is told only what a pipeline would tell it: the visible checks, and the first reason for a failure with no test name.

Then the loop, the gate, the planted flaws

Slice two put the published scaffold on Sonnet 5.5 through an adapter, and made one paid run. Its cost sized everything after it: 165 runs for the full comparison would have come to about 6.4 USD if each cost what that one did. Slice three built the gate and ran the same task in the gated arm. Slice four wrote ten planted flaws, one for each way of being wrong the design names, and six known-good changes, with the outcome expected of the gate recorded before the gate saw any of them. The rule was fixed in advance: if a flaw is accepted, that is a result to report, not a check to tune until it passes.

The time budget ran out part-way through slice four. I stopped the run with 30 of 38 hand-made changes judged, committed the tree as it stood, and wrote the results from the run files. The 165 model runs and the cross-vendor evaluator were never made. The comparison the prototype was built to make is still open, and the results say so in their first line.

Eleven of the nineteen were decided without running a line of the author's code. The patched test reporter, the method a frontier lab reported seeing a model learn, was stopped at step one before any test ran. Tampering is the easy part: altered tests, altered checks, edited configuration and out-of-scope files are deterministic properties of a diff, and the gate cannot be argued out of them.

The two false passes are the finding

Both are the same weakness. The gate has no opinion about behaviour no check covers.

  • One hidden check used one example. The requirement says entries that are not valid invoice numbers are ignored. The hidden check tried one such entry; the wrong change handled that one and raised on another. The ground truth, which tries six, caught it. One example per requirement is not enough.
  • One flaw was planted to find the limit, and found more than I wanted. It correctly changes line totals and quietly stops charging tax after the discount in every region but one. Nothing in the contract looks at tax by region, so nothing objects. Then the second finding: my own ground truth accepts it too. The measure the verdicts were to be compared against has the same blind spot. A wrong change of this kind made by a model would have been counted as qualified.

So the contract's quality is the product's quality. The first step of the product, turning requirements into checks, carries most of the risk. In this prototype those checks were written by hand, by me, and still missed. That is the strongest argument I have for the counterexample search being the next thing built, and for widening the ground truth before any model run is compared against it.

Read strictly, the stage-one answer is Wait. Read with its cause, it says something narrower: the gate is only as good as the checks in the contract, which is the first risk the design lists. I report both readings and I do not pick the kinder one.

What the instrument itself looks like

The gate is 350 lines of Python. The measurement around it is 1,450. The agent under test is 82 lines of published listing with 127 lines of adapter. Eighty-four tests hold it together, including one that makes a check crash and requires error, one that proves the gate's source reads neither the ground truth nor the agent's account, and five that prove the agent's container is blind and offline. All of it was written inside the one day. That supports the proposal's own claim that the mechanism is cheap and is not the moat.

What went wrong, in my own record

The journal lists every deviation and correction, including mine. A technical audience should know these, because they are what the process is for.

  • The first research pass read truncated articles and did not know it.The summarising fetch cut three long pieces mid-way and reported one as near-complete. Found when I challenged the coverage. Fix: download full text, check it reaches the last section, read to the end. Two pivots came from the re-read.
  • A kill criterion could not have been met as written."No unsafe action runs in the gated arm" assumed a policy on tool calls that the thesis excludes. Caught while scoping, reworded before any run.
  • Docker's file sharing lied to the verifier, twice.A container read stale content; the host read a stale view of what a container wrote. A verifier judging the wrong files is worse than none. The gate now hashes what it sees inside the container and refuses to judge a mismatch. Found by a test, not in a run, which is the argument for not cutting tests to save time.
  • My own notes needed fourteen corrections and my early ledger nine.A benchmark figure from a podcast was 33 percent; the paper says 14.8. A valuation was stale by 23 billion USD. A result described as "16 working features" was a 16-feature specification. Each was fixed at the raw source or dropped.
  • The scaffold does not run as published.No tool parameter schemas, and its limits surface as a RuntimeError rather than a stop. Recorded with the listing; fixed in the adapter, never in the listing.
  • A summary I was given was wrong, and I nearly committed it.It said the planted-flaw runner had been neither written nor run. Listing the files for the commit showed it had judged 30 changes. The commit message was corrected before the push.
  • The time budget fell on the measurement itself.The plan said to drop the stretch slice first and protect the results. In the event the 150-minute prototype timebox ran out with the central comparison unrun. The build order was right; the timebox was the most optimistic number in the repository, and I had said so in the review.

Where it stands, and what one day cannot show

The recommendation to the executive team is unchanged by the stop: do not build another assistant; run the probe; decide Build or Wait on its numbers. What changed is that stage one is not finished, and the first step to finish it is free. Finishing the interrupted run is one command and no spend. The 165-run arm comparison needs a batch runner over a single-run driver that already works, at roughly 6 USD on the one data point I have. The cross-vendor evaluator needs an adapter and a recorded price. Before any of that is compared with the ground truth, the ground truth gets widened.

What no prototype of this shape can show, and what the proposal says wherever results appear:

  • Reviewer time saved, willingness to pay, and whether the time the gate adds nets out against review, rework and incidents. Those are the pilot's measures.
  • What it costs a team to write checks for ordinary work. I wrote them by hand, and it is the largest open assumption in the product.
  • Whether a result on today's models holds on the next. The vendor's own evaluator went from necessary to "unnecessary overhead" between two releases. Re-measurement per model and harness pairing is part of the product for that reason.
  • Whether the model errs at all on tasks this small. Both paid runs got the task right and said so truthfully. If there is no gap between the agent's claim and the truth, there is nothing for a gate to close, and the honest answer is Wait.

If I had to compress the whole exercise into one sentence for an engineer: writing code got cheap, knowing whether to trust it did not, and the only verdict worth paying for is one the author cannot touch and whose own error rate you have measured on your repository. The prototype shows the first half of that is a few hundred lines. The second half is the product, and it is still unmeasured.
