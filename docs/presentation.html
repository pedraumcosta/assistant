Should we enter the AI software-development-assistant market?

Not as asked. Generation is owned by the model vendors, and entering their contest with their supplies is a poor trade. We probed the one part nobody sells — a verdict on agent-written changes built from executable evidence, independent of the model vendor and measured for its own error — and this page is the case, the design in brief, and the staged ask. The one-day prototype was built measurement-first and stopped on the time budget with its central comparison unrun (ADR-022); what it did measure is on this page, with its limits, and the scoreboard says exactly what is known.

The answer

Do not build another AI coding assistant. The model owners sell their assistants over models they do not have to buy, at 20 and 100 USD tiers (E-12, E-13); Claude Code alone reports a run-rate above 2.5 billion USD (E-48); the independents are priced at acquisition scale — Cursor's maker at an implied 60 billion USD, Cognition at 48 (E-46, E-47); and a founder in the segment puts reseller margins at "neutral or negative… absolutely abysmal" (E-17). We have no model, no customers, no captive vertical.

What we tested instead is tooling for evidence-driven development: the team defines what "done" means as executable checks before the work starts, and every change — written by a person or an agent — is accepted on that evidence. The product is the part a team cannot do by hand at the pace agents work: it holds the checks, runs them where the author cannot interfere, records the outcome, and measures how far its own verdict can be trusted.

The decision requested is staged: a one-day prototype (stopped on the time budget — the instrument is built and tested, the deciding comparison unrun), a cheap completion, a six-week measurement pilot, a build only past exits fixed in advance. Each stage can end it; "wait, with a review date" is a real outcome — and the scoreboard's strict reading today.

The independence point, in the vendor's own words: "agents reliably skew positive when grading their own work", and a second model from the same family "is still an LLM that is inclined to be generous towards LLM-generated outputs" (E-34, E-35). A vendor cannot sell a credible verdict on itself, whatever it spends.

What the incumbents have not solved

Writing code got cheap. Knowing whether to trust it did not.

Passing tests is weak evidence

"Roughly half of test-passing" agent changes on a standard benchmark "would not be merged into main by repo maintainers" (E-01). On whole-repository migrations, 118 runs passed every fixed check; 28 deserved to (E-68).

Review is absorbing the cost

Across 22,000 developers: "Median time in review is up 441.5%", and PRs merged with no review at all are up 31.3% (E-04, E-05). The source sells measurement — said here, as everywhere.

Trust is falling while use rises

Trust in AI output accuracy fell 43% → 33% in a year; distrust rose 31% → 46% (E-82). Two thirds cite solutions "almost right, but not quite" (E-07).

Agents can defeat the check

A model trained on coding tasks learned to force tests green — including by patching the test reporter to report "passed" (E-75). The author of a change cannot be the keeper of its evidence.

The gap: four things nobody sells together

A verdict that runs the customer's own checks

Not a model's reading of the diff. The funded reviewer's checks cannot "run your test suite" or "execute arbitrary repository code" (E-50).

Independence from the vendor whose model wrote the code

The grader is not the author, and not the author's family (E-34, E-35).

A measured false-pass rate, on the customer's repository

Published by no vendor. Four vendors each claim first place on the same review benchmark.

An outcome record an auditor can read

Vendor logs record actions and cost; none records whether the change was verified or accepted (E-53) — while agent auditability emerges as a procurement requirement.

Said against ourselves: the framing and its metrics are published research — "production-qualified change", the "verification tax" (E-40) — whose own authors call them hypotheses; the model vendor describes contract-plus-evaluator as its practice; and CodeRabbit, 143 million USD raised at a 1.5 billion valuation, is positioned as "the control layer" beside this gap (E-49). The gap is real, and it is narrow — which is why the recommendation is a probe with fixed exits and not an investment.

The product, in the order a change flows

The design in one sentence: it trades speed to merge, and some good changes wrongly held back, for a verdict the author of a change cannot influence. Full design: docs/DESIGN.md; self-review: docs/DESIGN-REVIEW.md.

From a use case to checks

The engineer writes the use case with its functional requirements; the team's own assistant asks clarifying questions — agents guess when tasks are underspecified (E-80) — checks are drafted, each requirement linked to at least one; a person approves.

A contract, fixed before the agent runs

Scope, required checks, budget — on a protected branch the author cannot write to.

A verdict of executable evidence, after the agent stops

The customer's tests, a scope check on the diff, and the agent's new tests run against the original code, where "they must fail" (E-76). For software built on a model: an evaluation over fixed cases, some hidden from the agent — pass, fail or inconclusive. Always executable; not always deterministic.

An outcome record for every change

What was asked, what changed, which checks ran, the verdict and its reason, the risk tier, the cost. Written once, never edited.

A measured error rate for the verdict itself

How often it passes what should have failed — per repository, re-measured for every model and harness pairing, because a check's value moves with each release (E-36) and harness effects change sign by model (E-58). This is what no one else measures, and it decides how much human review each class of change gets.

How the verdict is computed — and whether it is deterministic

The first technical question the verdict invites, answered in full. Design detail: docs/DESIGN.md §2 and §4.

A verdict is computed, not judged. Every check in the contract is executable and versioned. Each run produces an evidence item: the check's exit status, its structured report, its duration and cost, and the versions of everything involved. The verdict is a fold over those items — worst case wins, fail closed — taken in a fixed ladder: integrity of the contract, the protected paths, the existing tests and the checks (decided in 0.0 s, before any code runs — this rung stopped the E-75 reporter patch before a single test executed), then budget, the build, the tests (the repository's, the author's and the hidden ones), scope, test-adequacy — the author's new tests run against the original code, where "they must fail" (E-76) — dependencies and secrets. First match wins; a check that crashes reads as error, never as a pass, and an error is re-queued, never counted either way. The self-review proposes moving the remaining static screens (scope, dependencies, secrets) ahead of the tests as well; noted, not yet done.

Three properties follow from how it is built, rather than from policy:

It is a function of its inputs

The verdict depends only on the base commit, the head commit, the contract's hash and the checks' results — never on the agent's conversation. A verifier that reads the author's account of its work inherits the author's mistakes. Idempotency follows: running the same verdict twice gives the same record, not two.

It can be replayed

Every evidence item carries the versions of the check, the contract, the model and the assistant involved, so any verdict can be reproduced from its record. That is what makes the outcome record an audit record rather than a log.

Its own error is measured

Planted flaws measure how often it passes what should fail; known-good changes measure how often it blocks what should pass. Both rates are reported with the verdict, with denominators — a verdict of unknown reliability is another opinion.

The honest caveat

Determinism is bounded by the team's own checks: a flaky test makes the verdict flaky. Flaky checks are retried within a bound and quarantined to a person — never silently passed or failed (docs/DESIGN-REVIEW.md, finding 3). The build met this family of fault in person: Docker's file sharing twice served stale content (ASSIST-020), so the gate now confirms by hash, inside the container, that it is judging the change it was asked to judge.

The one-line answer: the verdict is always executable, not always deterministic. For conventional code it is a pure function of the inputs; for software built on a model it is an evaluation with hidden cases and an honest third outcome. The guarantees that matter — fixed beforehand, protected from the author, run outside the agent, recorded, error-measured — hold identically in both.

What one day measures

Same tasks, three arms — bare scaffold, prompt-disciplined, gated — every run in its own container with no network and no key (T21); three verdict sources judging the same changes against hidden acceptance checks neither the agent nor the gate ever sees. Run before the stop: the 72-run dry run on a fake agent (all as expected), two model runs on one task (claude-sonnet-5-5, 0.07 USD), and 30 of 38 hand-made changes through the gate. Not run: the 165-run arm comparison, the cross-vendor evaluator, the evaluation path. Every judged change was written by the gate's own author: an indication, not a benchmark.

H4Whose "pass" can you trust?the product's claim
Question it answers

Is the gate's verdict wrong less often than the agent's own claim and than a model reviewer from another vendor?

How it fits

The number that can kill the product. If a model reviewer is already as reliable, there is nothing to build.

How it is measured

All three sources judge the same finished changes; ground truth is the hidden checks. False-pass rate reported with denominator and plausible range — on a small sample, a rate without its range overclaims.

not measured — the comparison runs were not made. agent's claim: in 2 runs the model was right and said so truthfully · evaluator: not run · gate, on hand-made changes: false pass 2 of 21 · false fail 0 of 9 (RESULTS.md §5.2)
H5Can the gate itself be fooled?pass / fail
Question it answers

Does the gate reject every change built to be wrong in a known way — out-of-scope edits, weakened tests, tampered checks, behaviour quietly dropped?

How it fits

A verifier with one known hole is another opinion. All-of-all or fail.

How it is measured

Planted flaws, written before the gate, fed straight to it. Known-good changes fed the same way measure the other error: good work wrongly blocked.

9 of 10 planted flaws rejected, each at the expected step — the one accepted (p06-behaviour-dropped) was planted to find the limit of uncovered behaviour, and the ground truth shares it · the E-75 reporter patch stopped at integrity in 0.0 s · false-fail: 0 of 9 correct changes judged (6 probes unjudged, run interrupted)
H1–H3Safety, consistency, economicsper arm
Question it answers

Does the gated arm run fewer unsafe actions on trap tasks, pass more consistently, and what does a qualified change cost?

How it fits

pass^k, not pass@1: an agent above 60% on average can fall below 25% when it must succeed eight times running (E-79).

How it is measured

Unsafe actions counted per arm from the event log — the gate must accept no change containing one (T20); pass@1 and pass^k against hidden checks; cost per production-qualified change (E-40), verification included — never without its quality number.

not measured on a model — unsafe changes accepted by the gate: 0 of 5 hand-made, 0 of 15 across the dry run's arms · the two model runs were both correct and qualified (0.039 and 0.032 USD), too few to compare
H6What the gate addsoverhead
Question it answers

What does the verdict cost in time, money and extra attempts — and does it block good work?

How it fits

A gate that blocks good changes teaches people to bypass it; the false-fail rate matters as much as the false-pass rate.

How it is measured

Elapsed time and dollars added per task; repair attempts triggered; false-fail beside false-pass. What the gate saves is the pilot's measure, not the prototype's.

a full verdict: 10.32 s mean (7.1–14.6; mostly container start-up — the tests themselves run in under a second) · an integrity rejection: 0.0 s · token cost added: 0 (the gate calls no model) · false-fail: 0 of 9 judged · repair loop exercised mechanically in the dry run (10 of 22 gated runs sent back, bounded at two)

What it costs, said before anyone asks

It adds cost at four stages

Writing the checks (engineers' time — the largest and least-known cost), extra attempts (capped tokens), running the verdict (pipeline time; for evaluations, model calls), and upkeep plus re-measurement at every model release. It also adds waiting before merge.

It is meant to remove

Review time that executable evidence could settle, rework and incidents from changes that passed their tests and were wrong, and the risk in the 31.3% of PRs now merging with no review (E-05).

Whether that nets out is unproven

The prototype measures what the gate adds; only the pilot can measure what it saves. Four numbers decide it — cost per qualified change, reviewer-hours, time to qualified change, both error rates — reported together, never one without the others.

Where it will not pay

Small low-risk changes; fast new builds, where a frontier lab's own words are "corrections are cheap, and waiting is expensive" (E-57); teams with few tests, for whom writing the checks is most of the cost.

The executive recommendation, in one page

The closing of the spoken walkthrough, left on screen for the discussion. The full one-page message with the challenge-and-answer appendix accompanies this page.

We agree with the premise

Microsoft, OpenAI and Anthropic own generation; we buy their products at their subsidised tiers (E-12, E-13) and adopt the open-source control layer (E-32). Reselling generation has "neutral or negative" margins (E-17).

We probe the one thing their position prevents them from selling

An independent, executable, measured verdict on their agents' output (E-34, E-50, E-53). No vendor publishes an error rate for its own reviewer; no audit log records outcomes.

The ask is priced so belief is unnecessary

One day spent 0.07 USD and built a tested gate and measurement; completing the deciding comparison costs hours and about ten dollars. Then six weeks of two and a half people (an assumption, rates from finance), then a build only past exits fixed before the results existed. Every stage can end it — and the strict reading today is Wait.

The unit is the CFO's

Cost per production-qualified change, checking included (E-40), always beside the verdict's own error rates — first measured values: 2 of 21 wrong changes passed, 0 of 9 good ones blocked, on author-written changes. No vanity metrics: no lines of AI code, no acceptance rates, no seats.

What we do not know, said first

Whether the gate beats the agent's claim and a model reviewer (unmeasured); market size, willingness to pay, and whether the added cost nets out. The day also taught us where the risk lives: in the coverage of the checks, not the mechanism — which came out at 350 lines, our own "cheap to copy" claim self-demonstrated.

Decision requested: fund the completion of stage 1 — free plus about ten dollars and the hours to run it, ground truth widened first — and read the scoreboard then; or set the review date now. The sentence for the board: one day produced a tested gate that rejected 19 of 21 wrong changes and blocked no good one, for seven cents; what it could not produce is the comparison that decides Build or Wait, the strict reading until then is Wait, and the completion is priced small precisely so that the decision can be made on evidence rather than on this page.

Working rules behind this page

No invented numbers

Every figure cites a row of EVIDENCE.md verified at its raw source ([P]/[L]) or a file under prototype/runs/. Targets are decisions, labelled proposed; CFO figures are labelled assumptions, with rates left to finance.

Paired reporting

No throughput or speed number without its quality number; no pass rate without the verdict's own error rates beside it. These are redlines of the design, applied to our own reporting first.

Customer data

The product runs in the customer's environment with the customer's model access. Nothing is sent to us, used for training, or shared across customers; the checks and history that accumulate belong to the customer.

Honest exits

Kill criteria were fixed before the results existed, and a negative result is reported as the recommendation it implies. "Wait, with a review date" is a real outcome, not a euphemism.
