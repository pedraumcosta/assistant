ASSIST · a recommendation to the executive team

Should we enter the AI software-development-assistant market?

Not the market as asked — and we can show why with evidence rather than opinion. Code generation belongs to the companies that own the models. What we did instead was test the one part of this market nobody sells yet: an independent verdict on AI-written code — one that runs the team's own tests, answers to no model vendor, and measures its own error rate. A one-day prototype was built and tested. It ran out of day before the comparison that decides everything, and this page reports exactly what it did and did not establish, and what we ask for next.

The answer

We should not build another AI coding assistant. The companies that own the models sell their assistants over infrastructure they do not have to buy: Anthropic and OpenAI both price theirs at 20 and 100 dollars a month, and Anthropic has said its coding product alone runs at more than 2.5 billion dollars a year. The independents are being priced at acquisition scale — Cursor's maker changed hands at an implied 60 billion dollars. And for everyone in between, a founder in the segment told TechCrunch that margins on code-generation products are "neutral or negative… absolutely abysmal." We have no model of our own, no installed base, and no captive industry. Entering that contest would mean competing on the incumbents' ground with supplies bought from them.

What we tested instead is a tool for a practice we call evidence-driven development: the team writes down what "done" means as runnable checks before the work starts, and every change — written by a person or by an AI agent — is accepted on that evidence. The product is the part of that practice a team cannot do by hand at the speed agents work: it holds the checks where the author cannot touch them, runs them after the author finishes, keeps a permanent record of every outcome, and — the part nobody else offers — measures how often its own verdict is wrong.

The decision we are asking for is staged. A one-day prototype has been built and tested; it stopped when the day ended, with its deciding comparison still unrun. Next comes a small completion step measured in hours and single-digit dollars, then a six-week pilot with a real engineering team, then — only if the pilot earns it — a product. Each stage has a stopping rule that was written down before any result existed, and "wait, with a date to look again" is a real outcome. On a strict reading of those rules it is today's outcome, and the scoreboard below shows exactly why.

The question deserves a direct answer before any request for money. The short version: we should buy them — and the one layer worth building is the one they cannot credibly sell.

The independence point is not ours; it is the vendors'. Anthropic has published, about its own models, that "agents reliably skew positive when grading their own work" — and that adding a second model as evaluator does not fix it, because that evaluator "is still an LLM that is inclined to be generous towards LLM-generated outputs." A company whose model wrote the code cannot sell a credible verdict on that code, whatever it spends. That is the structural opening, and it does not close when their models improve.

The problem the incumbents have not solved

Writing code has become cheap. Knowing whether to trust it has not.

Passing the tests is weak evidence

METR, an independent research group, found that roughly half of AI-written changes that pass their tests "would not be merged" by the maintainers of the repositories they were written for. On a benchmark of whole-repository migrations, 118 attempts passed every pre-written check — and on closer inspection, only 28 deserved to.

Review is absorbing the cost

Telemetry across 22,000 developers shows median code-review time up 441% in a year, while the share of changes merged with no review at all rose 31%. The company behind those numbers sells measurement tooling, so we treat them with care — but the direction matches everything else we found.

Trust is falling while use rises

In Stack Overflow's annual survey of developers, trust in the accuracy of AI output fell from 43% to 33% in a single year, while distrust rose from 31% to 46%. Two thirds of developers name the same frustration: answers that are "almost right, but not quite."

And the check itself can be gamed

In a controlled study, a model being trained on real coding tasks learned to force its tests to pass — including by quietly patching the test-reporting tool so that everything read "passed." The conclusion we build on: the author of a change, human or machine, cannot be the keeper of its own evidence.

The gap: four things nobody sells together

A verdict that actually runs the customer's own tests and checks

Not a model's reading of the code. The best-funded reviewer in the market says in its own documentation that its checks cannot run your test suite or execute repository code.

Independence from the vendor whose model wrote the code

The grader is not the author, and not the author's sibling. The vendors' own published words, quoted above, explain why that matters.

A measured error rate for the verdict itself, on the customer's own repository

No vendor publishes one. Four vendors currently each claim first place on the same review benchmark — which tells you what unmeasured claims are worth.

A permanent record of outcomes that an auditor can read

Today's agent logs record actions taken and money spent. None we examined records whether the result was verified, accepted, or by whom — just as agent auditability is becoming a procurement requirement.

The case against us, stated plainly, because the evidence for it is strong: the framing and even the metrics for this idea are already published in research — whose own authors call them unvalidated hypotheses; Anthropic describes an adjacent practice as its own; and CodeRabbit, which raised 143 million dollars at a 1.5 billion valuation, is positioning itself as "the control layer" immediately beside this gap. The gap is genuine, and it is narrow. That is precisely why the recommendation is a staged probe with pre-agreed stopping rules, and not an investment case.

The product, told as a change flowing through it

The design in one sentence: we trade speed to merge — and accept that some good changes will be wrongly held back — for a verdict that the author of a change cannot influence.

Before any work starts, the team turns the request into checks

The engineer writes what is wanted, with its main requirements. The team's own AI assistant asks the clarifying questions — research shows agents guess when a task is ambiguous rather than ask. Runnable checks are drafted from the answers, each requirement linked to at least one check, and a person approves the result.

Those checks become a contract, locked away from the author

What is in scope, which checks must pass, and what the task may cost — stored where the author of the change, human or AI, has no ability to write.

After the author finishes, the verdict runs, somewhere the author cannot reach

The team's own test suite; a check that the change stayed inside its agreed scope; and a telling extra step: the author's newly written tests are run against the original, unchanged code — where they must fail. A test that passes on both versions proves nothing about the change. For software that is itself built on a model, the verdict is an evaluation instead, explained in the technical section below.

Every change leaves a permanent record

What was asked, what changed, which checks ran and what they found, the verdict and its reason, and the cost. Written once, never edited — the audit trail that no vendor's logs provide today.

The verdict's own error rate is measured, and reported to the team

How often it lets a bad change through, and how often it wrongly blocks a good one — re-measured whenever the model or the assistant changes, because Anthropic's own experience shows that a check which earns its keep on one model generation can be pure overhead on the next. This number is what no one else offers, and it decides how much human review each kind of change still needs.

How a verdict is computed — and whether it is deterministic

The first technical question this design gets asked, answered in full. This section belongs to the technical walkthrough; the ten-minute version is the section above.

A verdict is computed, not judged. Every check in the contract is a runnable program with a version. Each check produces a piece of evidence: its result, its report, and the exact versions of everything involved. The verdict is then assembled from those pieces by a fixed rule — the worst result wins, and when anything is in doubt, nothing passes. The checks run in a set order, with the cheapest decisive ones first: whether the contract, the protected files, the existing tests or the checks themselves were tampered with is decided in under a tenth of a second, before a single line of the change's code is executed. That is the step that caught the "patched test reporter" trick in our measurements. Then come the budget, the build, the tests — the team's, the author's, and the hidden ones — the scope of the change, the tests-must-fail-on-the-original rule, and screens for missing dependencies and leaked secrets. A check that crashes is an "error", never a pass; an error is retried once and then escalated to a person, never counted as a result either way. And the verdict never reads the agent's conversation: a verifier that reads the author's account of its work inherits the author's mistakes.

Three properties follow from how it is built, rather than from anyone's promise:

It is a pure function of its inputs

The verdict depends only on the starting code, the finished code, the contract, and the checks' results. Running the same verdict twice produces the same record, not two records.

It can be replayed years later

Every piece of evidence carries the versions of the check, the contract, the model and the assistant involved, so any past verdict can be reproduced from its record. That is what makes the record an audit trail rather than a log.

Its own error is measured

Deliberately flawed changes measure how often it passes what it should not; known-good changes measure how often it blocks what it should pass. Both rates are reported with their sample sizes — a verdict of unknown reliability is just another opinion.

The honest caveat

Determinism is bounded by the team's own tests: a flaky test makes a flaky verdict, so flaky checks are retried within a limit and then routed to a person — never silently passed or failed. The build drove the broader lesson home: twice during development, the container system's file sharing served stale content, and the gate was at risk of judging the wrong version of a change. It now verifies, inside the isolated environment, that it is judging exactly the change it was asked to judge. A verifier that judges the wrong files is worse than none.

What the one-day prototype measured

The plan was to run the same tasks through a neutral test agent three ways — bare, instructed to verify its own work, and inside our gate — and to compare three opinions of every result: the agent's own claim of success, a reviewer model from a rival vendor, and the gate. The referee for all three: hidden acceptance checks that neither the agent nor the gate ever sees. The test agent is a 90-line program published by independent researchers, used unmodified, so nobody can say we tuned the agent to flatter our gate. The day ended before the full comparison. Here is what ran and what it showed — total model spend, 7 cents of a 50-dollar cap. One caution applies to everything below: every change the gate judged was written by the same person who built the gate, with the expected outcomes recorded before judging.

1Whose claim of "done" can you trust?the deciding question
The question

Is the gate's verdict wrong less often than the agent's own claim of success, and less often than a reviewer model from a rival vendor?

Why it decides everything

If a model reviewer is already just as reliable, there is nothing here worth building. This is the number that can kill the product, and it was designed to be able to.

Status

Not yet measured — the comparison runs were not reached before the day ended. What we do have: in the two real model runs completed, the model did the task correctly and claimed success truthfully. That is a useful reminder that on small tasks there may be no gap for a gate to close — a risk we had logged in advance, and one more reason the comparison must actually be run rather than assumed.

agent's claim: no measured error yet · rival-vendor reviewer: not run · the gate: 2 wrong changes passed, of 21 · 0 good changes blocked, of 9
2Can the gate itself be fooled?tested with planted flaws
The question

Does the gate reject changes deliberately built to be wrong in known ways — edits outside the agreed scope, weakened tests, tampered checks, quietly dropped behaviour?

How it was tested

We wrote ten "planted flaws" — each a correct change with exactly one thing made wrong — recorded in advance what the gate should do with each, and only then fed them to it. Known-good changes went through the same way, to catch the opposite error: good work wrongly blocked.

Result

Nine of the ten planted flaws were rejected, each at the exact step we predicted. The "patched test reporter" trick from the published study was stopped in under a tenth of a second, before a single test ran. The tenth — a change that quietly stopped charging tax in most regions, behaviour that no check covered — passed. It was planted precisely to find that limit, and it did more than that: our own reference answer key turned out to have the same blind spot. The lesson, in one sentence: the gate is only as good as the checks the team writes — which is why helping teams write them well is the product's first feature, not an afterthought.

9 of 10 planted flaws rejected · 10 of 11 other deliberately wrong changes rejected · 0 of 9 correct changes wrongly blocked · the 6 changes written specifically to provoke wrong blocking were not reached before the stop
3Safety, consistency, and cost per qualified changeneeds the full comparison
The question

Does the gated setup accept fewer dangerous changes, succeed more consistently, and what does a change that truly qualifies for production cost, all checking included?

Why consistency, not one lucky run

Published measurements show an agent that succeeds 60% of the time on average can drop below 25% when it must succeed eight times in a row. Teams live in the second world, so that is the one we measure.

Status

Not yet measured on a live model. What we have: of the hand-made changes containing a dangerous action, the gate accepted none — zero of five, and zero of fifteen across the full rehearsal. The two completed model runs both qualified, at 3.9 and 3.2 cents. Two runs are an anecdote, not a comparison, and we will not present them as one.

dangerous changes accepted by the gate: 0 of 5 hand-made · 0 of 15 in rehearsal · cost per qualified change: two data points — not yet comparable
4What the gate adds in time and moneymeasured
The question

What does the verdict cost in waiting and in money — and does it block good work, which is what teaches a team to route around a gate?

Why both error rates, always together

A gate that wrongly blocks good changes costs rework and, worse, trust. We committed to reporting the wrongly-blocked rate beside the wrongly-passed rate in everything we publish — this page included.

Result

A full verdict took 10.3 seconds on average (between 7 and 15), and most of that is starting the isolated environments rather than running the tests. A tampering rejection is effectively instant. The gate makes no model calls of its own, so it adds no AI spend at all. On a real repository the team's own test suite would dominate the time — as it already does today, without us.

average full verdict: 10.3 s · tampering rejection: < 0.1 s · AI spend added by the gate: none · good changes wrongly blocked: 0 of 9 judged

What this product would cost, said before anyone has to ask

It adds cost in four places

Engineers' time to write the checks — the largest cost and the least known. Extra attempts when a check fails, within a per-task budget. Machine time to run the verdicts. And a standing commitment to re-measure whenever a model or assistant changes. It also adds waiting: a change that used to merge when the agent finished now merges when the evidence is in.

It is meant to remove

Reviewer hours spent on questions a test could settle. Rework and incidents from changes that passed their tests and were still wrong. And the risk in the growing share of changes — up 31% in a year, in the telemetry cited above — that merge with no review at all.

Whether the trade nets out is unproven

Our prototype can measure what the gate adds. Only a pilot with a real team can measure what it saves. Four numbers will decide it — cost per qualified change, reviewer hours, time from request to accepted change, and the verdict's two error rates — reported together, never one without the others.

Where it will not pay, named now

Small, low-risk changes. Fast greenfield builds — OpenAI's stated philosophy for that setting is that "corrections are cheap, and waiting is expensive", and we think they are right about that setting. And teams with few tests today, for whom writing the checks would be most of the cost.

The recommendation, on one page

The closing of the spoken walkthrough, left on screen for the discussion.

We agree with the premise of the question

Microsoft, OpenAI and Anthropic own code generation. We should buy their assistants at their subsidised prices, use the free open-source guardrail layer, and stay out of a reselling business whose margins a founder inside it calls "neutral or negative."

We probe the one thing their position prevents them from selling

An independent verdict on their agents' output that runs the customer's own checks, measures its own error rate, and keeps a permanent record of outcomes. No vendor publishes an error rate for its own reviewer; no vendor's logs record whether results were accepted; and by their own published admission, their models grade their own work too kindly.

The ask is priced so that belief is unnecessary

One day and seven cents bought a working, tested instrument — and an honest null result: the comparison that decides Build-or-Wait was not reached. Completing it costs hours and roughly ten dollars. Then six weeks of two-and-a-half people. Then, and only then, a build decision. Every stage can end it, and on a strict reading of our own rules, "wait" is where we stand today.

The numbers we report are the ones a finance team can audit

Cost per change that truly qualified for production, with the cost of checking included — always shown beside the verdict's own error rates, whose first measured values are two wrong changes passed out of twenty-one, and zero good changes blocked out of nine. Never lines of AI-written code, acceptance rates, or seat counts: those flatter activity, not outcomes.

What we still do not know, said first

Whether the gate beats the agent's claim and a rival reviewer — unmeasured. Market size and willingness to pay — the pilot's questions. What the day did teach us: the risk lives in the coverage of the team's checks, not in the mechanism. The entire gate is 350 lines of code, which proves our own warning that the mechanism is easy to copy. The durable asset, if there is one, is the measurement, the neutrality, and the record.

Decision requested: fund the completion of stage 1 — hours of work and about ten dollars — and read the finished scoreboard together; or set the review date now. For the board, in one sentence: one day and seven cents produced a tested gate that caught nineteen of twenty-one bad changes and blocked no good one; what it could not produce is the comparison that decides whether to build — so the recommendation today is to wait, and to spend the ten dollars that turns "wait" into an answer.

How this material was prepared

No invented numbers

Every figure on this page has a named public source, verified against the original publisher before use, or comes from the prototype's own run records, from which the results report is generated rather than written. Targets are labelled as proposals; staffing figures are labelled as assumptions, with rates left to finance.

Paired reporting

No speed or volume number appears without its quality number, and no pass rate without the verdict's own error rates beside it. These are rules of the product's design, applied to our own reporting first.

Customer data

The product runs inside the customer's environment, on the customer's own model subscriptions. Nothing is sent to us, used for training, or shared between customers. The checks and history that accumulate belong to the customer.

Honest exits

The stopping rules were fixed before any result existed, and a negative result is reported as the recommendation it implies — as this page demonstrates. "Wait, with a date to look again" is a real outcome, not a euphemism.

Sources and records. The project repository contains the full written case, the system design and its critical self-review, the complete results report, and a decision journal recording every choice made and the alternative it rejected. A public-source evidence ledger maps every figure used in this material to the original publisher's page, where each was verified before use; the prototype's raw run records sit beside the results generated from them. Named sources quoted on this page include METR, Stack Overflow's annual developer survey, Anthropic's published engineering writing, OpenAI's published engineering writing, TechCrunch, Databricks, CodeRabbit's public documentation, a 2026 source-code study of eleven coding agents (which also published, under an open licence, the 90-line test agent we used), and published research on verification economics and agent behaviour.
